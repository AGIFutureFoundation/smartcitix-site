What Immersive Practice Can and Cannot Teach

AR/VR Training for the Union Trades

Status — read this first. SmartCiti.X is W3 Series 10 in the "33 named W3 sub-series in the April 2026 internal list." The proposed issuer is a new, separate corporation, SmartCiti.X, Inc. — a Delaware C-corporation is recommended, Wyoming is the alternative, and whether it is a public benefit corporation or a standard corporation is one choice for counsel. It is not incorporated, filed, funded, or offered. Nothing in this article is a promise that a training programme, cohort, or facility exists today. The learner platform described here is built locally as a demonstration; its scenarios are simulations, not certification, licensure, or apprenticeship hours. No union, employer, city, or site is named as a partner anywhere in this article, because no signed agreement exists.

A learner puts on a headset, reaches for a virtual panel, and runs a fault-finding exercise that would take a week to set up in a real workshop — and that, done wrong in the real world, could hurt someone. Ten minutes later the same learner does it again, makes a different mistake, and the simulation shows exactly where the sequence broke down. That is the honest promise of immersive training for the skilled trades: not a replacement for the workshop, but a place to fail safely, repeat endlessly, and arrive at the real equipment already knowing the shape of the job.

The question is not whether VR works. It is what it works for.

The debate about virtual reality in training is usually staged as a contest: VR versus the classroom, VR versus the workshop, one winner. That framing is the first mistake. Nobody serious proposes replacing the workshop. The researchers who study immersive training most carefully keep arriving at the same conclusion: VR is a powerful supplement, and a poor substitute. The question a training programme has to answer is narrower and more useful — which parts of learning a trade belong in a simulation, which parts belong only in the real world, and how do you hand a learner from one to the other without losing them in the gap? It is also a promise that has been oversold. Virtual reality cannot teach the weight of a conduit bender, the heat coming off a brazed joint, or the judgement of a worker who has felt a panel hum and known something was wrong before any meter said so.

Start with what a headset is genuinely good at. It is good at sequence: the order of operations in a procedure, which step comes before which, and what happens when you skip one. It is good at hazard recognition: spotting what is wrong in a scene before you touch anything. It is good at repetition without cost: the twentieth conduit bend costs no copper, the tenth misdiagnosis costs no refrigerant, the failed lockout costs no one an injury. It is good at confidence — a learner who has already run a procedure five times in simulation walks up to the real panel steadier than one who has only read about it. And it is good at access: a learner in a place with no training hall can still rehearse the shape of the work.

None of that is a trade. A trade is what happens when the sequence meets the material — when the conduit resists, the joint leaks, the reading does not match the book, and the person next to you is counting on you to notice. The rest of this article keeps both halves in view.

What the evidence actually shows

The research on immersive training is young, uneven, and often funded by the companies that sell the headsets — which is a reason to read it carefully, not a reason to ignore it. The honest picture, taken across the strongest studies, is encouraging in some places and cautionary in others, and a programme that reports only the encouraging half is not being honest with its learners.

The best-known study in the field is PwC's 2020 VR soft-skills training efficacy study, which compared the same course delivered as classroom training, e-learning, and VR to new managers across twelve US locations. VR learners completed training up to four times faster than classroom learners — and still three times faster after accounting for headset setup time — and were up to 275% more confident in applying what they had learned. They were also 3.75 times more emotionally connected to the content than classroom learners, and VR reached cost parity with classroom training at 375 learners. These are striking numbers, and they are also numbers about a soft-skills course on inclusive leadership, taught to managers, not about electricians bending conduit. They establish that immersive learning can be faster and more engaging — they do not establish that it teaches a trade. Any programme that quotes PwC's "four times faster" without the soft-skills caveat is borrowing credibility it has not earned.

The evidence closer to the trades is smaller and messier, which is exactly why it is worth reading. In 2022, the National Center for Construction Education and Research (NCCER), together with Associated Builders and Contractors of Illinois, ran a pilot study in which two classes performed the same two assigned tasks — circular saw and electric drill training. One class worked in a traditional lab; the other incorporated VR learning. Both classes received instruction, an instructor demonstration, and guided practice, and an expert evaluator assessed each learner against the same rubric. The VR class scored 75% against 53.46% for the traditional class, and 100% of the VR class secured employment after training, against 69% for the non-VR class. Those numbers come to us through Transfr, the VR company whose simulations were used in the pilot, so they should be read as a vendor-reported pilot result, not as independent proof — but a pilot with an expert evaluator and a shared rubric is a real signal, and it is the kind of signal a programme should replicate with its own learners rather than merely quote.

A stronger, independent result comes from Switzerland. In a joint project by the ZHAW School of Management and Law and the Zurich University of Teacher Education (PH Zurich), researchers built a VR environment in which apprentice electricians practised testing an electrical installation in a virtual garage. A field study compared 68 Swiss assembly electricians and electrical installers in their final year of training: 35 prepared for the practical exam with VR, 33 with traditional methods. Assembly electricians in particular benefited, improving in their final exam by almost a whole grade compared with learners without VR. The researchers' own emphasis matters as much as the result: they stressed that VR should in no way replace real-life practical work and contact with trainers, and that learners in complex technical processes — real or virtual — need structured support, which is why their simulation included a help and question system alongside game elements.

And then there is the study that should keep every VR enthusiast honest. Thomann, Zimmermann and Deutscher (2024), in a randomised controlled trial with 72 vocational students in warehouse logistics, published in Computers & Education, found that immersive VR did not improve immediate declarative knowledge acquisition — the paper-and-pencil group actually outperformed the VR group on an objective post-test. What VR did improve was motivation, immersion, and students' perceived knowledge gains. The gap between feeling that you have learned and actually having learned is precisely the trap a training programme has to design against. Feeling confident after a simulation is not the same as being competent at the bench, and a programme that measures only confidence will congratulate itself while its learners fail their first real task.

Taken together, the evidence supports a specific, modest claim: immersive practice improves engagement, confidence, procedural familiarity and — in the best-designed studies — measured performance on practical tasks, when it is blended with real practice and structured coaching. It does not support the claim that VR alone teaches a trade, that it replaces instructors, or that confidence after a simulation equals competence. The SmartCiti.X learner platform is built on the modest claim, not the grand one.

What a headset cannot teach

It is worth being specific about the limits, because vague scepticism is as useless as vague enthusiasm. Here is what a headset, as the technology stands today, cannot teach a trades learner.

Weight and resistance. A conduit bender fights back. A torque wrench clicks at a threshold your wrist learns to feel. A brazing torch has a heft and a balance that no controller reproduces. Haptic feedback in consumer headsets is a buzz in the hand, not a load on the arm. The muscle memory of the trades is built against real resistance, and it can only be built where the resistance exists.

Heat, smell, and sound. A seasoned HVAC technician can hear a compressor struggling and smell a burnt winding before any gauge confirms it. Simulations can show a temperature number; they cannot teach the sensory vocabulary that experienced tradespeople actually use to diagnose. That vocabulary is learned standing next to someone who has it, over years.

Consequence. In a simulation, a mistake costs a reset. In a panel, a mistake can cost a hand. That difference is not a flaw in simulation — it is the entire point of it — but it means simulation cannot teach the respect for consequence that is the core of trade safety culture. It can teach the procedure that embodies that respect; it cannot teach the feeling. Only working with real energy, under a real supervisor, does that.

The job site. A training bay is not a job site. Job sites have weather, dust, other trades in your way, a foreman changing the plan at 7 a.m., and a schedule that does not pause while you think. No simulation today reproduces the social and logistical texture of real work. The closest a programme can come is to put its learners on real sites, supervised, as early as it safely can.

Judgement under ambiguity. Simulations have right answers; real faults do not always. A diagnosis that reads clean on three gauges and still leaves a customer without cooling is a judgement problem, not a procedure problem. Judgement grows from seeing many real cases, including the ones that do not fit the book, with a journeyman who can explain what they noticed and why.

The learner platform: six scenarios, thirty steps, one rule

The SmartCiti.X learner platform, built locally and verified by its own check script, is a small demonstration of that philosophy. It contains six scenarios, each of five steps, for thirty steps in total, across two tiers — fundamentals and applied — and eleven skill strands. The scenarios are: K-12 AR/VR foundations (measure, tools, safety); union trades electrical (panel and conduit install); union trades HVAC (diagnose, braze and commission); robotics human-robot integration in a shared cell; safety procedures (hazards, PPE, fit, report); and outreach prevention and school screening. Each scenario runs in three modes — VR headset, desktop WebGL, and mobile touch — so that a learner without a headset is not locked out of the curriculum. Every scenario ends the same way: a debrief with the coach.

Two design choices in the platform deserve attention, because they are the choices that separate a training tool from a demo. The first is that safety is not a module; it is the spine. Safety appears as a strand in all six scenarios, and procedure in all six — the two most-taught strands in the platform, ahead of tools, machines, coordination and documentation at two scenarios each, and materials, layout and troubleshooting at one each. Two registry strands, inspection and leadership, have no scenario yet, which the platform records openly rather than hiding. A learner cannot reach the electrical panel without a PPE check and a verify-dead step, cannot reach the brazing bench without a recovery plan, and cannot finish any scenario without reporting. That ordering is deliberate: the habit the platform is trying to build is not "do the task and remember safety," it is "safety is the first step of the task."

The second choice is the honesty labelling. Every scenario file states, in its own text, that the work is simulation only — not certification, not licensure, not an apprenticeship credential. The HVAC scenario states that EPA Section 608 certification is an external exam and that the run does not certify. The electrical scenario states the same about licensure. The outreach scenario states that screening is not diagnosis: flag, refer, and record — never diagnose in the field. The dashboard that sits alongside the scenarios runs in demo mode only, on twenty anonymised sample events for five fictional learners, under a banner that always says "Sample data, not real learners." A training tool that lies about what it is cannot be trusted about what it teaches.

The platform also makes a quieter point about scope. Its six scenarios do not pretend to cover a trade. They cover the beginnings: foundations for school-age learners, one electrical sequence, one HVAC sequence, one robotics integration sequence, the safety procedures that precede every task, and one outreach sequence. A real curriculum would need dozens of scenarios per trade, written with journeymen, tested with learners, and revised against real outcomes. The platform is a proof that the structure works — scenarios, steps, checks, strands, tiers, a viewer — not a claim that the curriculum exists.

How a serious programme would use VR

If the evidence says "blend," then the interesting question is what the blend looks like in practice. A serious programme — and SmartCiti.X, if it is ever built, would have to be one — would use immersive practice in at least five distinct roles, each with its own guardrails.

First, as orientation. Before a learner touches a real panel, they should have walked the bay in simulation: found the safety station, the first-aid point, the extinguisher, the report desk. The platform's safety scenario does exactly this, and its K-12 foundations scenario does it for younger learners: meet the safety station, learn to measure, identify tools, follow a procedure in order. Orientation in VR is cheap, repeatable, and — for a nervous first-day learner — far less intimidating than orientation on a live floor. But orientation is not training; it is the doorway to training, and a programme that counts it as a completed unit is inflating its numbers.

Second, as procedure rehearsal. The electrical scenario's five steps — PPE check and verify-dead, read the schematic, bend the conduit, land the conductors, test and debrief — are a procedure in miniature. Rehearsed in simulation, the sequence becomes automatic, which frees the learner's attention on the real bench for the things that actually need attention: the feel of the bend, the torque on the lug, the reading on the meter. This is where the Swiss study's result makes sense: VR improved exam performance because the procedure was already in the learner's head, leaving their hands and eyes free to work. Procedure rehearsal is the strongest, best-evidenced use of VR in the trades, and it is the use a programme should invest in first.

Third, as hazard training. Some of the most important things a tradesperson must recognise are the things you hope they never experience: an arc flash, a refrigerant release, a robot moving unexpectedly. Simulation is the only safe place to meet those hazards. The platform's safety scenario has the learner name the hazards on the bay's hazard board before touching any tool, and its robotics scenario makes confirming the cell boundary and the e-stop the first step of every run. That ordering mirrors the law. Under OSHA's personal protective equipment standard, employers must train each worker who is required to use PPE to know when it is necessary, what kind is necessary, how to put it on, adjust, wear and take it off, the limitations of the equipment, and its proper care, maintenance, useful life and disposal — and must provide the PPE itself at no cost to the worker. A simulation can teach the knowledge half of that standard; only supervised real work, with real PPE that fits, can teach the rest. The standard also requires a written hazard assessment and retraining when a worker's knowledge or use shows they have not retained the skill — both of which are programme responsibilities no headset can discharge.

Fourth, as diagnosis practice. The HVAC scenario is built around diagnosis: read pressures, temperatures and airflow together, and name the diagnosis from all three readings before touching a torch. Diagnosis is a cognitive skill — pattern recognition under uncertainty — and it is exactly the kind of skill that benefits from many cheap repetitions with varied cases. A simulation can present a learner with fifty faulty systems in a week; a real shop can present five. But the simulation's cases are authored, and authored cases have a tell: they contain the fault the author put in. Real faults are messier, and the programme must say so to its learners, or it will produce diagnosticians who are confident until the first fault that is not in the book.

Fifth, as assessment — carefully. A simulation can record every step a learner took, every check they passed or skipped, every second they spent. That is a powerful assessment record, and the platform's learner-progress contract is designed to capture exactly that: structured events, validated against a schema, with duplicate events rejected. But a simulation assessment measures simulation performance. It is evidence about a learner, not a verdict on them. A programme that treats a VR pass as a certification is making the same error as a programme that treats a written exam as proof someone can wire a panel. The honest assessment model is triangulation — simulation records, instructor observation on real equipment, and supervised work — with no single leg carrying the whole weight.

What "delivered" should mean

SmartCiti.X's planning documents set a gate for the second tranche of its proposed funding: an AR/VR pilot "delivered" at the proposed Louisiana site, among other conditions including a signed union agreement, a first site closed or leased, a first manufacturing order or pilot customer, site staff hired, and the first tranche reconciled with a Windfall report drafted. That gate is only as strong as its definition, and "delivered" is a word that can mean almost anything — a headset in a box, a demo for visitors, a curriculum in use. If the gate is ever to mean something, it needs a written definition, agreed before the money moves, that answers at least these questions.

How many learners, and who are they? A pilot that trains twelve volunteers from the project team proves nothing about training a cohort. A pilot should name its learner population — and it should include learners who are not already enthusiasts, because enthusiasts will succeed with any tool.

What was measured, and against what? A pilot should measure performance on real equipment after simulation, not only performance inside the simulation, and not only self-reported confidence. The NCCER pilot's design is the model: the same tasks, the same rubric, an expert evaluator, a comparison group. Without a comparison, a pilot can show that learners improved and still prove nothing, because learners improve with any instruction.

What did the coach do? A pilot that works because a brilliant coach carried it is a pilot of the coach, not of the technology. The pilot should record the coaching model — how much instructor time per learner, what the instructor did when a learner got stuck — so that the result can be reproduced without the same person in the room.

What failed? A pilot report that lists no failures is a marketing document. Headsets break, learners get motion sick, the software crashes at the worst moment, the Wi-Fi in the building cannot carry the load. The pilot should report all of it, with numbers, because the cost of fixing those failures is part of the true cost of the programme.

And what happens next? A pilot is not a programme. Its report should say what would change before a second cohort — which scenarios to rewrite, which steps confused learners, which strands need a scenario that does not exist yet — and who decides. The platform's own open questions point in this direction: hall mapping is open, real VR station IDs are not yet assigned, and the anonymisation rules for any real cohort log are unwritten. Those are not embarrassments; they are the honest inventory of a demonstration. A pilot's job is to turn that inventory into a plan.

Costs, access, and who gets left out

There is a version of the VR training story in which the technology democratises access: a headset is cheaper than a workshop, so training can reach places that could never afford a hall. There is also a version in which it concentrates access: the schools and programmes that can afford headsets, software licences, IT support and a room with enough floor space pull further ahead, while the rest fall behind. Both versions are true somewhere, and a programme has to choose which one it will be.

The costs are real and often undercounted. PwC's finding that VR reaches cost parity with classroom training at 375 learners is a useful anchor, but it is about a soft-skills course delivered at enterprise scale, and it counts the cost of delivery, not the cost of building a trades curriculum. A trades programme must author its scenarios with journeymen, maintain the software, replace broken headsets, sanitise shared equipment, train its instructors, and keep a real workshop running alongside the virtual one — because the virtual one does not replace it. The platform's three-mode design — headset, desktop, mobile — is one answer to the access question: a learner without a headset can still run the same scenario on a phone or a laptop. That is a design value worth keeping, and worth extending: no learner's progress should depend on hardware they cannot afford.

There is also the question of who the training is for. The platform's scenarios include a K-12 foundations scenario and an outreach scenario, which points at a wider ambition than training adults for jobs: introducing young people to the trades early, and taking screening and prevention work into schools and communities. That ambition is attractive, and it is also where the evidence is thinnest. The Swiss study was about apprentices in their final year; the NCCER pilot was about adult learners in a lab. There is little rigorous evidence about VR training for school-age learners in the trades, and a programme that extends into schools should treat that as research to be done, not as a claim to be made.

Finally, there is the question the Foundation's own materials raise and do not yet answer: whose curriculum is this? The platform's scenarios are hand-authored, and their hall mapping is explicitly open — no hall is mapped to any scenario. The SmartCiti.X bundle's own README states that zero of the 111 halls in its roster have signed off by a named practitioner, and a named hall is not a partner. That is the right standard, and it applies to curriculum as much as to partnership: a training programme for the union trades cannot be written by a software team alone. It has to be written with the people who do the work, who set the standards, and who will employ the graduates — and until they have signed off, in writing, the curriculum is a draft. The Tranche 2 gate requiring a signed union agreement is, in this light, not a bureaucratic hurdle. It is the mechanism by which a draft becomes a programme.

The bottom line

Immersive practice can teach sequence, recognition, diagnosis, and confidence — and the best evidence says it can teach them well, faster, and more engagingly than a classroom alone. It cannot teach weight, heat, consequence, judgement, or the job site. A programme that knows that — simulation for rehearsal, real equipment for craft, coaches throughout, assessment that triangulates — is worth building. A programme that forgets it will produce confident graduates who fail their first real task, and the failure will be blamed on the learners.

SmartCiti.X, as a plan, has the beginnings of the honest version: a small platform that labels its own limits, puts safety first in every scenario, and refuses to call simulation certification. What it does not yet have is the rest — a formed corporation, a signed union agreement, a site, a cohort, or a pilot measured against a comparison group. Those are not details. They are the programme. Until they exist, the most accurate thing to say about SmartCiti.X's AR/VR training is that the demonstration is built, the evidence is encouraging and incomplete, and the real work — the curriculum, the coaches, the agreements, the measurement — is still ahead.

Frequently asked questions

Does VR training replace hands-on workshop time?
No. The research consistently treats VR as a supplement. The Swiss researchers who found the strongest positive result for apprentice electricians stressed that VR should in no way replace real-life practical work and contact with trainers. Simulation rehearses sequence and decision-making; the workshop teaches the material reality of the trade.

Is there proof VR training works for the trades?
There is encouraging evidence and no settled proof. A 2022 NCCER pilot found VR learners scored 75% against 53.46% for a traditional class on the same tasks, and a 2023 Swiss field study found apprentice electricians improved by almost a whole grade in their final exam after VR preparation. But the NCCER result is vendor-reported, the Swiss study covered 68 learners, and a 2024 randomised trial found VR did not improve immediate declarative knowledge over paper-based methods. The evidence supports blended use, not replacement.

Can a simulation certify me as an electrician or HVAC technician?
No. The platform states this in every scenario: simulation only, not certification, licensure, or apprenticeship hours. EPA Section 608 certification is an external exam. A simulation can prepare you for those exams; it cannot grant them.

What does OSHA actually require for PPE training?
Under OSHA's personal protective equipment standard, employers must provide PPE at no cost to workers who need it, assess the workplace for hazards, and train each affected worker on when PPE is necessary, what kind is necessary, how to put it on, adjust, wear and take it off, the limitations of the equipment, and its proper care, maintenance, useful life and disposal. A simulation can teach the knowledge; only supervised real work with properly fitting PPE completes the training.

What would count as a delivered AR/VR pilot for SmartCiti.X?
That is not yet defined in writing, and it should be before any funding gate depends on it. A serious definition would name the learner population, measure performance on real equipment against a comparison group, record the coaching model, report failures honestly, and say what would change before a second cohort.

Is SmartCiti.X a real training programme I can enrol in?
Not today. SmartCiti.X is a proposed new corporation, not incorporated, filed, funded, or offered. The learner platform is a locally built demonstration. There is no cohort, no site, and no enrolment. Inquiries go to the Foundation at x@agifuturefoundation.org.

Glossary

  • AR (augmented reality): digital information overlaid on the real world, for example a schematic shown over a real panel.
  • VR (virtual reality): a fully simulated environment, usually experienced through a headset, in which the learner interacts with virtual objects.
  • JATC (Joint Apprenticeship Training Committee): a labour-management committee that runs a registered apprenticeship programme for a trade.
  • NCCER: the National Center for Construction Education and Research, which develops curricula and credentials for the construction trades.
  • PPE: personal protective equipment — gloves, safety glasses, hard hats, respirators and similar equipment worn to minimise exposure to hazards.
  • EPA Section 608: the section of the Clean Air Act that requires technicians who service equipment containing regulated refrigerants to be certified, and prohibits knowingly venting refrigerant.
  • Simulation: a modelled practice environment. In the SmartCiti.X platform, a five-step scenario with a check at each step. Simulation only — not certification.
